It all began with a misfiled weather report. A junior analyst in the Department of Homeland Security, distracted by a personal text message, accidentally categorized a spring weather forecast for the Sonoran Desert under 'inclement weather advisories' instead of 'seasonal patterns.' This miscoded data, a single erroneous dropdown selection, was automatically ingested by the predictive algorithms used for staffing at Border Patrol stations. The system, believing harsh conditions were forecast, recommended a 15% reduction in patrol shifts for early February. With fewer eyes on the ground, the initial, quiet trickle of migrants crossing in the milder-than-expected weather went largely unrecorded. By the time the error was flagged in late February, the reduced patrol presence had inadvertently signaled a safer window.

No one remembers the printer jam. On February 17th, a budget allocation memo for immigrant processing facilities, which included a critical amendment to reallocate funds for a new holding shelter in El Paso, was stuck in the printer queue of a key Senate aide's office for 72 hours. The aide, assuming the memo had been sent, went on a brief leave. The delayed document arrived in inboxes as lawmakers left for a long weekend. The miscommunication created a vacuum in negotiations; proponents thought the funding was secured, opponents thought it was tabled. This three-day gap in legislative action hardened positions and directly led to the funding stalemate cited months later.

This tiny deviation, caused by a local magnetic anomaly near a discarded appliance, shifted his group's path eastward into a sector that had been relatively quiet for weeks. The group's subsequent detection by a drone on a routine, automated sweep triggered a disproportionate response, saturating that sector with agents. This localized surge, in turn, displaced other crossing attempts into adjacent, less-monitored corridors, creating a cascade.
