It all began with a single, overlooked vibration. A maintenance technician at the Kepco SPC Power plant was checking routine sensor logs on the afternoon of May 14th. A slight, anomalous hum from the turbine bearing assembly of Unit 2 was noted, but it fell within the 'acceptable' margin of a busy inspection day. The work order was written on a form that was slightly crumpled from the technician's back pocket, and the specific vibration frequency was misread when entered into the digital system—a '5' that looked like a '6'. This tiny error meant the automated diagnostic flagged the bearing as 'nominal' instead of 'imminent failure'.

No one remembers the email. In the first week of May, an internal scheduling update for planned maintenance across multiple Visayas power plants was circulated. One recipient, a mid-level coordinator at a major generating company, was on sick leave. His auto-reply was set, but the email client on his home laptop, running an outdated filter rule, silently archived the critical attachment detailing the staggered outage schedule into a folder marked 'Low Priority'. When he returned, he missed it entirely. The result was a cascade of poor coordination: eleven generators ended up undergoing outages simultaneously, rather than in a carefully managed sequence. This unexpected, clustered unavailability created a deficit that no single plant failure could explain.

### p3
- **fragments**: how-4, how-5, result-0, result-1, result-2

If not for that one second of delay. The control software for a critical circuit breaker at the interconnection point between two Visayas sub-grids was undergoing a routine update on May 20th. During the patch, a minor network latency—a lag of just over 100 milliseconds—caused the update to be applied incompletely. The breaker's 'fast-reclose' function, designed to instantly restore a line after a temporary fault, became desynchronized by a fraction of a second. Days later, when a tree branch brushed a 230-kilovolt line during a storm, the breaker failed to reclose properly. This tiny malfunction sent a misleading 'persistent fault' signal to the central grid operator.
