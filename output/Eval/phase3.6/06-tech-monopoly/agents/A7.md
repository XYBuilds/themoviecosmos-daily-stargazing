A junior engineer, updating the ranking weights for shopping results, accidentally left the 'boost' factor for the internal comparison service slightly too high. This tiny numerical error, a few decimal points in a line of code meant to be temporary, created a feedback loop. Over months, user clicks on the prominently displayed, but not necessarily superior, internal services reinforced the algorithm's bias. No one remembers the exact meeting, but the consequence was clear. A mid-level product manager, in a weekly sync, casually suggested changing the color of the 'Book' button for the internal flight service from a subdued grey to a vibrant blue—just for a small A/B test in one EU country. The test showed a minuscule, but statistically significant, uptick in clicks. This 'win' was replicated for hotel services, then shopping comparisons. Each change was too small to trigger a full antitrust review on its own. But the cumulative effect, year over year, was a measurable, systematic diversion of traffic. The Commission's investigation began not with a bang, but with a pattern emerging from years of these tiny, incremental UX tweaks.
